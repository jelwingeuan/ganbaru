# Flashcards alone won't pass the JLPT

Ganbaru is preparing learners for a stable exam. The JLPT is on paper, every item is multiple choice, and the format has not changed since December 2022. N1 and N2 have two timed papers and N3–N5 have three. Every level is scored from 0 to 180 on a scale built with Item Response Theory (IRT) and equated between sessions, and to pass, a candidate must reach the overall pass mark and also a minimum score in every scoring section. Since then the only change to results is a CEFR reference level printed for candidates who pass, starting December 2025. No computer-based JLPT has been announced, and the next sitting is 6 December 2026. Applications reached a record 1.94 million in 2025. About two in three candidates fail (the December 2025 pass rate was 33.9%), and seats in Japan for both 2026 sittings sold out weeks before the deadline. The only legitimate past papers are the two Official Practice Workbooks (2012 and 2018), each holding about one real test per level. jlpt.jp gives them away for personal study, but its terms bar re-hosting, exclude all listening audio from reuse, and require written permission for almost anything a paid app would do with them. On study methods, the strongest evidence supports spaced retrieval practice with corrective feedback, scheduled by FSRS, together with typed recall for readings and interleaving for items that are easy to confuse. In a benchmark of 350 million reviews, FSRS predicted recall better than the older SM-2 algorithm for 99.6% of users. Mnemonics and gamification help a little and can backfire. Prep apps fall into two groups: SRS "learning engines" with no exam realism, and "exam drillers" that have mock tests but draw complaints about accuracy. Learners' most costly problem sits between the two: clearing a review queue does not carry over to finishing the reading under a hard timer or catching audio that plays once. Ganbaru can own that gap with original items in the JLPT format, a strict exam mode, readiness shown per section against the official minimums, and an FSRS plan whose daily load is budgeted backward from the exam date.

## A format frozen since 2022, scored to punish any weak section

### Two papers at N1–N2, three at N3–N5

The JLPT tests reading, listening and the vocabulary and grammar behind them, and nothing else. Every item is multiple choice on a machine-scored answer sheet. Most items have four options and some listening items have three. There is no speaking or writing component ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html)). N1 and N2 put vocabulary, grammar and reading into one long paper, so every minute spent on a vocabulary item comes out of reading time. N3–N5 give vocabulary its own paper. The official reason is that lower levels have fewer usable items, and mixing them with reading passages would leak answers ([New JLPT Guidebook summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/reference/pdf/guidebook_s_e.pdf)).

| Level | Paper 1 | Paper 2 | Paper 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Vocabulary・Grammar・Reading, **110 min** | Listening, **55 min** (60 before Dec 2022) | — | 165 min |
| N2 | Vocabulary・Grammar・Reading, **105 min** | Listening, **50 min** | — | 155 min |
| N3 | Vocabulary, **30 min** | Grammar・Reading, **70 min** | Listening, **40 min** | 140 min |
| N4 | Vocabulary, **25 min** (30 before Dec 2020) | Grammar・Reading, **55 min** (was 60) | Listening, **35 min** | 115 min |
| N5 | Vocabulary, **20 min** (25 before Dec 2020) | Grammar・Reading, **40 min** (was 50) | Listening, **30 min** | 90 min |

*Source: [JLPT Test Sections and Test Times](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/guideline/testsections.html). Totals are sums of the official section times. Listening time varies slightly with the length of each session's recording.*

Since the 2010 redesign, timing or length has changed only twice. From December 2020, N4 and N5 lost minutes and items. N5 vocabulary, grammar and reading dropped from 67 to 43 items, and N4 from 70 to 57. The JLPT says difficulty and content stayed the same, and that the shorter tests still give reliable results ([N4/N5 change notice](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/topics/202009091599642827.html)). From December 2022, N1 listening went from 60 to 55 minutes and from 35 to 30 items ([N1 change notice](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/topics/202208051659677223.html)). The jlpt.jp news list shows no further format notice through 28 September 2026 ([jlpt.jp](https://www.jlpt.jp/)). Watch out when building from official documents. The 2010 English per-level PDFs still give the old times (N1 listening 60 minutes; N4 30/60/35; N5 25/50/30). Only the Japanese "_revised" PDFs are current ([n1_e.pdf, 2010](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/guideline/pdf/n1_e.pdf); [n1_revised.pdf](https://www.jlpt.jp/guideline/pdf/n1_revised.pdf)).

| Level | Vocabulary | Grammar | Reading | Listening | Approx. total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | 25 | 20 | 26 | 30 | **101** |
| N2 | 32 | 22 | 21 | 32 | **107** |
| N3 | 35 | 23 | 16 | 28 | **102** |
| N4 | 28 | 21 | 8 | 28 | **85** |
| N5 | 21 | 17 | 5 | 24 | **67** |

*Sources: approximate official item counts from the [Guidebook summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/reference/pdf/guidebook_s_e.pdf), the [2020 N4/N5 notice](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/topics/202009091599642827.html) and the [2022 N1 notice](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/topics/202208051659677223.html). Totals are sums.*

The mix of question types (大問) differs by level, and a mock-test generator has to encode it. All of the following comes from the official composition table ([JLPT test sections, JA](https://www.jlpt.jp/guideline/testsections.html)):

| Question type | Levels where it appears |
|---|---|
| Word formation (語形成) | N2 only |
| Orthography (表記) | Every level except N1 |
| Usage (用法) | Every level except N5 |
| Long-passage comprehension | N1 and N3 only |
| Integrated and thematic reading comprehension | N1 and N2 only |
| Listening: general outline (概要理解) | N1–N3 |
| Listening: verbal expressions, answered while looking at an illustration | N3–N5 |

The official passage lengths give timed reading drills a concrete target. Long and thematic passages are about 1,000 characters at N1, N2 thematic passages about 900, and N1–N2 information-retrieval material about 700. An N5 short passage is about 80 characters ([N1 specification](https://www.jlpt.jp/guideline/pdf/n1_revised.pdf); [N2 specification](https://www.jlpt.jp/guideline/pdf/n2.pdf); [N5 specification](https://www.jlpt.jp/guideline/pdf/n5_revised.pdf)). Store the item counts as ranges, not constants. The JLPT says counts vary slightly between sessions and that one reading passage can carry several questions ([Guidebook summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/reference/pdf/guidebook_s_e.pdf)). The N2 and N3 counts still rest on the 2009 guidebook because no change notice has ever been issued for them. The 2022 notice also gives an earlier N1 listening count of 35, while the 2009 guidebook gives 37. That points to an unannounced trim somewhere between those dates.

### Scoring: a total mark plus a floor in every section

| Level | Scoring sections (scaled) | Overall pass mark | Sectional minimums | CEFR level shown to passers (since Dec 2025) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Language Knowledge 0–60, Reading 0–60, Listening 0–60 | **100**/180 | 19/60 each | 100–141 = B2; 142+ = C1 |
| N2 | Same three sections | **90**/180 | 19/60 each | 90–111 = B1; 112+ = B2 |
| N3 | Same three sections | **95**/180 | 19/60 each | 95–103 = A2; 104+ = B1 |
| N4 | Language Knowledge・Reading 0–120, Listening 0–60 | **90**/180 | 38/120 and 19/60 | 90+ = A2 |
| N5 | Same two sections | **80**/180 | 38/120 and 19/60 | 80+ = A1 |

*Sources: [JLPT scoring sections and pass marks](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/guideline/results.html); [JLPT CEFR reference](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/about/cefr_reference.html).*

To pass, a candidate must reach the overall mark **and every sectional minimum**. A high total does not rescue one section below its line. Missing any section is an automatic fail, and the report then shows asterisks in place of section scores ([Pass or Fail](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/guideline/results.html)). Test papers and scoring sections do not line up exactly. At N3, the grammar items sit in the Grammar・Reading paper but count toward the Language Knowledge score ([Guidebook summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/reference/pdf/guidebook_s_e.pdf)). Scores are not counts of correct answers. The JLPT uses scaled scores based on Item Response Theory, which depend on each candidate's pattern of answers. The FAQ says this means a candidate with many correct answers can still get a fairly modest score ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html)). Scores are equated across sessions, so candidates of the same ability get the same score however hard a particular paper was ([Characteristics of the New JLPT](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/reference/pdf/guide2011_e_02.pdf)). The JLPT publishes neither its IRT model nor any raw-to-scaled conversion table, so **every third-party scaled score is an estimate**. One part of the score report can be reproduced exactly: the "reference information" bands. These are A for 67% or more correct, B for 34–66% and C for under 34%. They cover vocabulary and grammar at N1–N3, and vocabulary, grammar and reading at N4–N5, and they play no part in pass or fail ([Characteristics of the New JLPT](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/reference/pdf/guide2011_e_02.pdf)).

The CEFR display is the one real change to results since 2022. It was announced in July 2023, a standard-setting panel met in October 2024, and the launch was moved from July 2025 to December 2025 ([CEFR news, JA](https://www.jlpt.jp/cefrlevel/cefrnews.html)). Score reports for candidates who pass now map the total score to a CEFR level, as in the table above. Candidates who fail see an asterisk. There is no CEFR level per section, no C2, and no claim about speaking or writing, and pass/fail rules and test content are unchanged ([JLPT CEFR reference](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/about/cefr_reference.html)). As a result, the margin above the pass mark now shows on the report. An N3 pass at 95–103 reads A2, while 104 or more reads B1, and an N2 candidate needs 112 to be labelled B2. This conflicts with how visa rules are being reported. Unseen Japan reports that the Business Manager visa accepts any N2 pass as evidence of B2, yet the JLPT's own table labels N2 passes below 112 as B1 ([Unseen Japan](https://unseen-japan.com/jlpt-seats-japan-visas/)). The sources reviewed do not settle which reading the immigration authorities apply.

## Record demand meets one-in-three pass rates and sold-out seats

The JLPT runs twice a year, on the first Sunday of July and the first Sunday of December. The 2026 sittings are **5 July (held) and 6 December (upcoming)** ([JLPT test sections, smartphone page](https://www.jlpt.jp/sp/e/guideline/testsections.html)). Some overseas cities offer only one sitting a year ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html)). July 2026 results went online at 10:00 JST on 27 August and stay viewable until **17:00 JST on 31 October 2026**, four days after this report's date. That information comes from a search summary of the official page ([Results announcement](https://www.jlpt.jp/sp/e/guideline/results_online.html)). Paper score reports reach candidates in Japan in early September for the July test and early February for the December test. Overseas, the FAQ says early October and early March, while a summary of the results page says mid-October and mid-March ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html)). No date has been announced for December 2026 online results. By analogy with the seven-to-eight-week lag for July, late January 2027 is likely, but this is an inference.

Pass rates are almost flat across N1–N4, at 28–38%, with only N5 near 50%. The overall rate for all of 2025 works out to about 35%. A flat rate suggests that candidates tend to choose the level at the edge of their ability, so a learner who registers should expect failure to be the most likely outcome unless their preparation is better than average. The gap between Japan and overseas is striking. Candidates in Japan made up 46% of December 2025 examinees and passed N1–N3 at 24–26%, against 34–41% overseas. The data do not explain why. One plausible reason is that more Japan-based candidates sit the test for visa or job requirements, sometimes before they are ready, but that is an inference. The July 2026 applicant breakdown fits the same pressure. Applications in Japan rose 64.3% for N2 and 22.4% for N1, and fell 46.9% for N4 and 12.6% for N3 ([July 2026 summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/about/pdf/2026julypr.pdf)). This lines up with the early closure of N3 and N4 registration and with visa rules that now require N2 or B2. That causal link is an inference, not stated in the official release.

- A March 2025 notice banned phones and other electronic devices for overseas candidates.

- Copies of score certificates have cost a fee since April 2025 ([certificate notice](https://www.jlpt.jp/topics/202411281732776568.html)).
- A test-accommodations page appeared in February 2026 ([accommodations](https://www.jlpt.jp/application/sta.html)).
- The test network kept growing. December 2025 ran in 81 overseas countries and regions and 247 cities, adding Lithuania and the UAE ([Dec 2025 summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/about/pdf/2025decemberpr.pdf)). The smaller July 2026 sitting ran in 62 countries and regions and 182 cities, five of them new ([July 2026 summary](https://www.jlpt.jp/about/pdf/2026julypr.pdf)).
- On 28 September 2026, jlpt.jp posted a PDF describing Japanese-language tests other than the JLPT ([other Japanese-language tests](https://www.jlpt.jp/topics/pdf/Other_Japanese-Language_Tests_jp.pdf)). This fits the reported push to divert demand to other tests, though nothing official makes that link.

**No computer-based JLPT has been announced.** When the FAQ says "computer-scored", it means the paper answer sheet, and the news list through September 2026 has no CBT item ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/index.html)). The strain on the system is documented in the press, not in JLPT policy. For July 2026, N4 and N3 registration in Japan closed on 25 and 27 March, against a 7 April deadline ([Sakigake/Kyodo](https://www.sakigake.jp/news/article/20260413CO0056/)). Immigration policy drives the demand. According to secondary reporting ([Unseen Japan](https://unseen-japan.com/jlpt-seats-japan-visas/); [ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本語能力試験)):

For a learner, failing means waiting six months, or a year where the test runs once a year, with no guaranteed seat at the next sitting.

## Two free official workbooks are the only legitimate past papers

Since the 2010 redesign, the Japan Foundation (JF) and Japan Educational Exchanges and Services (JEES), which run the JLPT, have released real test questions in exactly one form: the Official Practice Workbooks. Volume 1 (2012) and Volume 2 (2018) each hold about one test's worth of questions per level, selected from tests actually given after 2010 ([Official Practice Workbook page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/sampleindex.html)). Both are complete and free on jlpt.jp, with no registration. Each level has four test booklets (vocabulary, grammar, reading, listening), one MP3 per listening question type, a sample answer sheet, an answer key and a listening script. That makes **ten official tests in total, two per level**. Bonjinsha sells the same content in print, with a CD, at ¥700 plus tax per level ([JLPT book information](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/reference/books.html); [Bonjinsha, N1 Vol. 2](https://www.bonjinsha.com/goods/detail?id=12862)). There is no third volume. As of October 2026, the book page, the workbook page and the 2023–2026 news lists mention only these two ([2026 news list](https://www.jlpt.jp/topics/list2026.html)).

| Official material | What it contains | Caveats |
|---|---|---|
| Official Practice Workbook, Vol. 1 (2012) | About one real post-2010 test per level, N1–N5, as PDFs and MP3s, with answer key and script | Listening audio is third-party content. The point-comprehension (Q2) audio was replaced without notice in 2017 |
| Official Practice Workbook, Vol. 2 (2018) | Same structure as Vol. 1 | Listening audio and several N1/N2 reading and grammar items are third-party content. No braille edition |
| New JLPT Sample Questions (2009) | Samples of every question type, written before the new test launched, with PDFs, MP3s, scripts and keys ([page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/sample09.html)) | Item counts and N1/N4/N5 times are out of date. Audio covers only some items. Print edition ¥900 / ¥800 plus tax |
| "Let's Try Sample Questions!" pages | One interactive sample per question type for each level ([page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/forlearners.html)) | Built with HTML5 canvas, so nothing to download. May differ from real booklets |
| Teacher slide ZIPs | PowerPoint and MP3s, one sample per question type taken from the 2009 set ([page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/reference/forteachers.html)) | Same material as the 2009 set |
| Braille files | 2009 guidebook, 2012 workbook and 2009 samples ([page](https://www.jlpt.jp/tenji.html)) | 2018 volume not available |

The 2009 collection uses today's question types but predates launch, so it is useful for practising question types, not as a timed mock test. The files are stable but not frozen. In May 2017, the links for the 2012 edition's Q2 listening were pointed at much larger files in a `sample2017` folder: 8.5–15.2 MB, against the 2.6–4.5 MB still printed on the page. The page also lists file sizes for the 2018 PDFs that are copied from the 2012 edition. Both discrepancies showed up in HTTP header checks during this research ([workbook page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/sampleindex.html)). An app that links straight to these files should re-check the links from time to time instead of trusting the page text.

The jlpt.jp Site Policy sets five tiers of permitted use ([Site Policy, EN](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/policy.html); [JA](https://www.jlpt.jp/policy.html)):

| Tier | Use | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Personal study, including copying, adapting and translating | Free |
| 2 | Quotation | Minimal, clearly marked off, secondary to the user's own work, and credited to the Official Worldwide JLPT Website with its URL |
| 3 | Copying for classes at non-profit schools | Teachers and students only |
| 4 | Reprinting and reproduction (転載・複製) for Japanese-language education | Pre-authorized, but only when nothing is charged for the resulting materials and the source is credited |
| 5 | Everything else | Prior written permission through the Inquiries form, stating purpose, media, publisher, price, print run and period of use |

**Third-party content is outside every one of these allowances.** All N1–N5 listening audio in both workbooks needs the original rights holder's permission, and so does a listed set of N1 and N2 grammar-in-text and reading items, for example N1 Q7–Q12 in Vol. 2 ([workbook page](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/samples/sampleindex.html)). Linking is welcome without notice, but the link must say it leads to the official site, and the site must not be framed to look as if it sits inside another one.

Anything sold as a recent "real" JLPT paper is off-limits. On 19 January 2024 the organizers stated that test questions and answers are never made public. They warned that disclosing, storing, sharing or using them can lead to nullified scores, withheld results, bans from future tests, notice to employers and schools, and legal action ([leak warning](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/topics/202401191708325175.html)). A Mainichi report, reposted on a forum, describes papers from a December 2024 N2 answer leak becoming impossible to grade, with fees refunded ([JREF repost](https://jref.com/threads/answer-leak-renders-jlpt-tests-ungradable.775049/)). Against that background, the iOS app Chopin JLPT claims to offer real past tests from July 2000 to December 2024 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/mt/app/chopin-jlpt-exam-preparation/id1360273755)), which cannot be reconciled with official policy. Kanshudo describes its timed tests as drawn from actual past JLPT tests ([Kanshudo](https://www.kanshudo.com/blog/jlpt)). The only real past items legitimately available are the workbook questions, and Kanshudo does not say whether it uses them with permission. Scanned copies of the workbooks also circulate on file-sharing sites ([pdfcoffee example](https://pdfcoffee.com/n3-a-pdf-free.html)). Books of real pre-2010 papers (the old 1級–4級 levels) are sold second-hand, but their format and scoring no longer match ([Yahoo! Shopping listing](https://shopping.yahoo.co.jp/products/ee25224c32)).

Read together, these terms leave a commercial app very little room. This is an interpretation, not legal advice. A product with subscriptions, in-app purchases, and arguably ads charges a fee, so tier 4 does not apply. Tier 4 also covers reproduction only, not adaptation (翻案), so reformatting or translating workbook items for a public app falls back to tier 5, written permission. The listening audio is excluded in every case. The policy does not say whether streaming a jlpt.jp MP3 inside an in-app player counts as the embedding that the linking clause forbids. That question is worth putting to JF/JEES directly.

None of the popular third-party sources is openly licensed:

- Shin Kanzen Master volumes often end with a full mock test ([Goodreads series](https://www.goodreads.com/series/188742)).
- 3A's mock-test series added a revised N3 Vol. 2 on 26 June 2025 ([Maruzen Junkudo](https://www.maruzenjunkudo.co.jp/products/9784883199761)).
- JLPT Sensei offers free downloadable practice tests that copy the official structure ([JLPT Sensei N4 test](https://jlptsensei.com/downloads/jlpt-n4-practice-test)).
- Bunpro's terms reserve copyright in its materials ([Bunpro terms](https://bunpro.jp/terms)).

All of these are fine for personal study and none can be reused in another app. This research found no openly licensed (Creative Commons) bank of JLPT-style questions. What is open is the raw material for writing original items:

## Spaced, effortful retrieval with feedback beats every alternative

### FSRS predicts forgetting best, though no one has measured the review savings

Spacing is one of the most robust findings in learning research. Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten study techniques. They rated distributed practice and practice testing as the only two of "high utility", and rated rereading, highlighting and the keyword mnemonic as low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Cepeda and colleagues studied more than 1,350 learners. The best gap between study sessions followed an inverted U, and as a share of the time until the test it shrank: from about **20–40% of a one-week delay to about 5–10% of a one-year delay** ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). For second-language learning specifically, Kim and Webb's meta-analysis found a medium-to-large spacing effect. Longer spacing paid off on delayed tests, and **equal and expanding schedules performed about the same** ([Kim & Webb 2022, via Western thesis](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)). Nakata's critical review likewise found no L2 vocabulary study showing an advantage for expanding intervals ([Nakata 2018](https://kansai-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/10256)), and his flashcard research found that the amount of spacing between encounters matters more than set size ([Nakata dissertation](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)). The practical lesson is that each review should be timed against how fast this learner is actually forgetting this item. A predictive model does that; a fixed ladder of intervals does not.

FSRS is the best-validated model of that kind that is openly available. The open srs-benchmark draws on about 727 million reviews from 10,000 Anki users and evaluates models on 349.9 million of them. On it, **FSRS-6 scores a log loss of 0.3460 and an AUC of 0.7034, against 0.4694 and 0.6369 for Duolingo's half-life regression model** (lower log loss and higher AUC are better). The newer FSRS-7 does slightly better again, at 0.3401 ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). **FSRS-6 beats SM-2 on log loss for 99.6% of users.** The benchmark's maintainers caution that SM-2 was never designed to output probabilities, and that a fully fair comparison between the two is impossible ([Expertium benchmark write-up](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)). Neural RWKV models predict better still, with a log loss of 0.2773, but they produce odd outputs, such as never predicting certain recall ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). Two limits matter for product claims. First, the benchmark measures prediction, not learning gains, and gives no "X% fewer reviews" figure. Claims of 15–40% savings that circulate online are unsourced ([CertFuel](https://www.certfuel.com/sie/study-guides/anki-vs-fsrs-sie/)). Second, Anki's documentation treats a desired retention of about 0.90 as the starting point, notes that higher targets raise the workload, and says FSRS needs some review history before it can fit a learner's personal parameters ([Anki FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm.md)). FSRS's academic grounding comes from MaiMemo's KDD 2022 and IEEE TKDE 2023 papers ([FSRS wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Algorithm)).

### Recognition builds meaning, typed recall builds readings, and feedback protects both

Retrieving an answer is itself what produces learning. Meta-analyses of the testing effect report effect sizes (Hedges' g) of about 0.50 (Rowland 2014) and about 0.61 (Adesope et al. 2017). Feedback reliably strengthens the effect: one summary of Rowland gives g = 0.73 with feedback against 0.39 without ([Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full); [StudyCards summary](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)). In Roediger and Karpicke's prose study, one study period followed by three tests gave about 61% recall a week later. Four study periods gave about 40%, even though restudying had won on a test five minutes after learning ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). Karpicke and Roediger then tested Swahili–English word pairs. Pairs that kept being tested after the first correct recall reached about 80% recall at one week, against about 35% for pairs dropped from testing, and extra restudy added little ([Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)). These classic figures were cited from their DOIs and not re-fetched during this research. The design lesson is clear all the same: **an item a learner has "mastered" should stay in the queue at long intervals, not be retired.**

The evidence on answer format conflicts until feedback is taken into account. Adesope found larger testing effects for multiple choice than for short answer, and Rowland found the reverse. A classroom study found a short-answer effect and no multiple-choice effect at all ([Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)). Kang, McDermott and Roediger resolve much of the conflict. With feedback, short-answer quizzes beat multiple choice. Without feedback, multiple choice did as well or better, because learners got too few short answers right in the first place ([Kang et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/09541440601056620)). Nakata found that recall formats build productive knowledge of spelling, while recognition is preferable when spelling is not needed ([Nakata 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)). That study used artificial Swahili–English pairs, and no study compares typed kana with multiple choice for Japanese readings specifically. The retrieval-effort hypothesis adds that harder retrievals which still succeed produce better retention ([Pyc & Rawson 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2009.01.004)). Multiple choice carries one specific risk. Roediger and Marsh found that it can plant wrong options as false knowledge, more so as the number of options grows ([Roediger & Marsh 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.5.1155)). This matters because the JLPT itself uses four options: drilling in its format without explaining the wrong answers can teach learners those wrong answers.

### Interleave the look-alikes; keep mnemonics and example sentences in supporting roles

Interleaving means mixing different item types within one practice session instead of practising each type in its own block. It helps where the skill being tested is telling similar things apart. Brunmair and Richter's meta-analysis of 59 studies found an overall g of 0.42, rising to 0.67 for visual categories such as painters' styles, and similarity between categories was the central factor ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). The same meta-analysis reportedly found no benefit, and possibly harm, for learning lists of words (g ≈ −0.39); that figure was not re-verified ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)). In mathematics, interleaved practice beat blocked practice on a delayed test, about 63% against 20% ([Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)). Interleaving therefore belongs where the JLPT tests discrimination:

- kanji that look alike
- near-synonyms, for the paraphrase items
- grammar forms that compete in the grammar-form items
- the different listening question types

It does not belong in first-pass learning of a vocabulary list.

The evidence for kanji mnemonics is thin. It amounts to two small Indonesian studies, with 36 students and with 34 students per group, both showing short-term gains ([UPI IJAL](https://ejournal.upi.edu/index.php/IJAL/article/download/15256/8830); [EUDL](https://eudl.eu/doi/10.4108/eai.5-8-2019.2289811)). The keyword method works in laboratory studies but is inconclusive in classrooms and over long delays ([e-FLT](https://e-flt.nus.edu.sg/?p=2012)). No large controlled trial has compared Heisig-style or WaniKani-style component mnemonics with conventional study. The often-quoted figure of 72% against 46% comes from a study of Russian vocabulary, not kanji ([Mojimori](https://mojimori.app/blog/science-of-efficient-kanji-learning-strategies/download)).

On context, the findings are as follows:

- Learning with first-language translations produced more words recalled than learning from context, though context transferred better to new sentences, mainly for more proficient learners ([Prince 1996](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1996.tb05468.x)).
- Adding a single glossed example sentence to word-pair learning gave little measurable gain ([Webb 2007](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168806072463)).
- Deliberate flashcard learning still built real, quasi-automatic lexical representations ([Elgort 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00613.x)).

Flashcards are a legitimate method. Example sentences earn their place as many varied encounters with a word, not as one fixed sentence.

### N2 to N1 costs more hours than the whole road to N3

| Level | Cumulative hours, kanji background | Cumulative hours, no kanji background | Added hours from previous level (no kanji, midpoints) |
|---|---|---|---|
| N5 | 250–450 | 325–600 | ~460 |
| N4 | 400–700 | 575–1,000 | ~325 |
| N3 | 700–1,100 | 950–1,700 | ~540 |
| N2 | 1,150–1,800 | 1,600–2,800 | ~875 |
| N1 | 1,700–2,600 | 3,000–4,800 | ~1,700 |

*Source: "JLPT Study Hour Comparison Data 2010–2015", attributed to a Japanese Language Education Center, via [Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/JLPT_Guide/About_JLPT). The last column is derived from it.*

This is the only structured estimate in wide circulation. Its method is undocumented, including whether the hours are classroom hours or self-study and how they were counted, and the JLPT itself publishes no hour estimates ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/)). A commercial school gives similar figures ([Coto Academy](https://cotoacademy.com/study-hours-needed-pass-jlpt-comparison-levels/)), while other guides go higher ([Migaku](https://migaku.com/blog/japanese/jlpt-study-schedule-guide)). Two patterns hold across these sources. First, the cost per level rises steeply: N2 to N1 takes more hours than the whole path from zero to N3. At an hour a day, a learner without kanji background faces roughly 8–13 years to N1. Second, the kanji advantage grows with level. Learners with a kanji background need about 75% of the hours at N5 but only 54–57% at N1. The N5 figure looks high next to learners' reports of passing within months, which suggests the table measures broad proficiency or classroom time rather than the minimum preparation needed to pass. Present these numbers as ranges, never as promises.

The JLPT stopped publishing official vocabulary, kanji and grammar lists in 2010, saying such lists were not necessarily appropriate ([JLPT FAQ](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/faq/)). Today's lists extrapolate from the pre-2010 specification. Old Level 4, which roughly matches N5, had about 103 kanji and 728 words, and old Level 2 about 1,023 kanji and 5,035 words. Estimates for the new levels put **N3 at about 650 kanji and 3,750 words and N1 at about 2,000 kanji and 10,000 words**. N3 is the least reliable estimate because no old level corresponds to it ([Tanos](https://www.tanos.co.uk/jlpt/aboutjlpt)). Going from N3 to N1 therefore means about 6,000 more words, or one to two years of new cards at 10–20 a day. Counts of grammar points vary too much between sources to quote. Bunpro alone lists more than 900 grammar points across all levels ([Bunpro](https://bunpro.jp/pricing)).

No experiment has tested JLPT-specific test-taking strategies. Common advice is practitioner lore: read the questions before the passage, skip hard items and come back, pre-read the listening options. The sectional minimums define the main way people fail. Kanji knowledge does nothing for listening, so learners from Chinese-speaking backgrounds, who can sail through vocabulary and reading, are the obvious group at risk of failing on listening alone. That is an inference from the scoring rules and the hours data, not a measured finding. The FAQ mentions no penalty for wrong answers, so answering every item is reasonable, but the scoring details beyond IRT are unpublished.

### Gamification lifts engagement modestly, and broken streaks backfire

Gamification helps, but modestly. Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis found effects (Hedges' g) of 0.49 on learning outcomes (19 studies, 1,686 participants), 0.36 on motivation and 0.25 on behaviour. Only the learning-outcome effect held up in the most rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). A 2026 meta-analysis of higher education reports a much larger d of 1.12 across 22 studies, but its results vary so widely between studies that design or publication bias is likely ([SAGE Open 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261421375)).

Specific mechanics cut both ways:

- **Mandatory badges and leaderboards.** In Hanus and Fox's 16-week course, these lowered students' intrinsic motivation and satisfaction compared with an identical course without them ([UCLA review of Hanus & Fox 2015](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)). In other online courses they made no difference to performance ([Arizona ITET](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/itet/article/download/18661/18409)).
- **Streaks.** Silverman and Barasch ran seven studies. **A broken streak made people less likely to continue, even when the break was not their fault.** Making the streak prominent magnified the damage, self-blame amplified it, and **letting people "repair" the streak reduced it** ([UDaily on Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)).
- **Rewards and feedback.** Tangible rewards that people expect and that depend on performance undermine intrinsic motivation, while informational feedback tends to support it ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)).
- **Streaks and competition in practice.** In a small study of Duolingo users, the streak was rated the most useful feature for vocabulary, and competitive features caused some users anxiety ([UKWMS repository](https://repositori.ukwms.ac.id/id/eprint/47113/)).

Duolingo's study of 225 learners reports reading and listening gains comparable to four university semesters for those who completed five units ([Duolingo](https://investors.duolingo.com/news-releases/news-release-details/leading-language-research-journal-publishes-study-showing)). However, Duolingo staff wrote it and it analysed only learners who completed the units ([CALL-EJ critique](https://callej.org/index.php/journal/article/download/419/347/1013)). Duolingo's half-life regression scheduler also trails FSRS on the open benchmark ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)), a reminder that heavy gamification and state-of-the-art scheduling are separate achievements.

## Learning engines retain, exam drillers simulate, and learners fall between

No tool covers the whole JLPT. SRS-first products (Bunpro, WaniKani, Anki, jpdb, Renshuu, MaruMori) track retention well but have historically lacked exam realism. Exam-first products (Migii, Todaii and a wave of small AI mock-exam apps) have mock tests but weaker teaching and frequent complaints about quality. The two camps are converging: **Bunpro added timed mock tests in September 2025 and Kanshudo in December 2025**.

Duolingo has no official JLPT alignment. Third-party estimates put a completed Japanese course at late N5 to early N4 ([noun.town](https://noun.town/blog/is-duolingo-enough-to-learn-japanese/)). jpdb.io is free and organised around the vocabulary of particular media ([Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-learning-resources-database/jpdb-io/)). Satori Reader offers graded reading at $8.99 a month with no JLPT mapping ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1382950847)).

The learner voice in this section comes from the WaniKani and Bunpro forums, App Store reviews, Hacker News and review sites. Reddit and Google Play could not be accessed, so how often each complaint occurs is unknown. Six complaints recur.

**Review pile-ups come first.** One Bunpro user calculated that 40 new lessons a day leads to roughly 500–800 reviews a day ([Bunpro forum](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/too-many-reviews-getting-out-of-hand/119297)). In June 2025 another reported review accuracy often below 70% under Bunpro's fixed intervals and asked for FSRS ([Bunpro forum](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/frustrated-with-the-srs-implementation-bunpro-feedback/134152)). WaniKani users describe facing hundreds of kanji reviews after a break ([Mezzoguild](https://www.mezzoguild.com/wanikani-review)). The cause is partly scheduling and partly pacing: nothing stops a learner from adding more items today than they can review later.

**The second complaint is the most costly: knowledge does not transfer to the exam.** A WaniKani forum member studied about two hours a day for two years with Genki and WaniKani, missed N5 by a couple of points, then got nearly the same result on N4. Replies urged them to study the test format itself and to work out whether the gap was knowledge or application ([WaniKani forum](https://community.wanikani.com/t/another-jlpt-failure-any-advice/60271)). After December 2025, N1 candidates reported barely finishing the reading, or running out of time on it ([WaniKani forum, JLPT 2025](https://community.wanikani.com/t/jlpt-2025/69343?page=12)). Listening is the most feared section. Poor audio at test venues made it worse; one venue held a CD player up to a microphone. One candidate was also thrown by spoken instructions in the local language rather than the English they had practised with ([WaniKani forum p.11](https://community.wanikani.com/t/jlpt-2025/69343?page=11); [p.12](https://community.wanikani.com/t/jlpt-2025/69343?page=12)).

**Third, mock tests are not realistic enough.** Bunpro staff said the timer is not enforced: going over the limit carries no penalty. Its listening audio can be replayed without limit ([Bunpro thread p.9](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/bunpro-jlpt-tests-new-feature-sep-20th-2025/149777?page=9)). Users found its listening harder than the real exam in one respect, because there is no time to read the options ahead and no pause after each question. They found the N2 test much easier than the real one ([Bunpro launch thread](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/bunpro-jlpt-tests-new-feature-sep-20th-2025/149777)). December 2025 candidates said the real exam was harder than any mock they had done ([WaniKani forum p.12](https://community.wanikani.com/t/jlpt-2025/69343?page=12)).

**The last three are paywalls, content accuracy and level lists.**

- **Paywalls.** Migii sells no plan shorter than three months and offers no trial ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1463267540)). Nihongo Pass puts its pass prediction behind its Pro tier ([Nihongo Pass](https://nihongopass.com/)).
- **Content accuracy.** A fluent reviewer found several mistakes in Migii's N1 questions. In September 2025 a lifetime buyer blamed suspected AI-generated content for misleading answers ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1463267540)).
- **Level lists.** Nothing authoritative splits the old Level 2 into N2 and N3 ([EDRDG mailing list](https://www.edrdg.org/jmdict_edict_list/2013/msg00045.html)), and learners notice tools giving the same item different levels ([Bunpro forum](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/bunpro-and-jisho-jlpt-levels/151355)).

Pass prediction is advertised but not validated. Nihongo Pass says it predicts JLPT scores from a learner's retention rate and shows a pass percentage, and Migii sells a prediction test. Neither publishes its method or any accuracy data ([Nihongo Pass](https://nihongopass.com/); [Migii prediction test](https://jlpt.migii.net/en/prediction-test)). The most transparent tool found is GyanMirai's calculator. It converts raw section scores into approximate scaled scores, says it tends to under-predict by 3–5 points, and calls itself a planning tool ([GyanMirai](https://www.gyanmirai.com/tools/jlpt-score-calculator)). No app publishes predicted results alongside actual ones. A predictor driven by SRS retention will overstate readiness for exactly the learners in the stories above about knowledge not transferring.

Comparing the products with the complaints gives a precise list of unmet needs:

- A strict exam mode: a hard timer, listening played once with official-style time to read ahead and pauses, and the correct section order and instructions.
- Readiness reported per section against the official minimums.
- A daily plan that caps the review load and schedules new items backward from the exam date. No product listing explicitly describes this.
- Timed reading drills sized to the official passage lengths. No surveyed tool offers these.
- FSRS for grammar as well as vocabulary, which Bunpro users have asked for ([Bunpro forum](https://community.bunpro.jp/t/frustrated-with-the-srs-implementation-bunpro-feedback/134152)).

Pricing sets the frame. Paid tools charge $5–9 a month, about $44–96 a year, or $110–350 for a lifetime licence. Free offerings such as Renshuu, jpdb and JLPT Sensei have taught learners to expect core study for free.

## Conclusion: build Ganbaru around exam transfer, not queue clearing

| Area | Design implication | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Content and licensing | Write every quiz and mock item from scratch in the official question types, built on JMdict, KANJIDIC, Tatoeba and OpenJLPT, with attribution and the ShareAlike condition respected. Link out to the official workbooks, opening on jlpt.jp and labelled as the official site; do not host or embed them. The bigger change is in what counts as progress. The JLPT scales scores with IRT and enforces a minimum in every section, so a learner's readiness is set by their weakest section under exam conditions, not by cards learned or streak length. Ganbaru's dashboard should therefore lead with section readiness measured in timed practice, and treat SRS statistics as inputs to it rather than the headline. The licensing constraint looks like a handicap but is a source of trust. Original items written to the published specification, honestly labelled levels, and a score estimate that shows its method are both the lawful route and the answer to the market's two loudest content complaints: AI-generated errors and opaque predictions.

The opening is narrowing, now that Bunpro and Kanshudo both have timed tests. Still, no product has shown that its mock scores predict real JLPT results. Ganbaru could be the first. It would invite users to share their official score reports after each sitting, with consent, and publish how well its estimates held up. December 2026 results are likely around late January 2027, and the B1/B2 immigration thresholds tighten from April 2027, so the value of an honest readiness estimate will keep rising.
